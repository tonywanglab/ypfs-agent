# Assessing the Scenario

Before prescribing action, it's worth separating the genuinely serious problem from the sensational framing.

## What actually matters here

The claim about "recursive self-improvement" and "without Nvidia chips" is dramatic, but the government's job isn't to adjudicate the AI research claims in real time. The concrete, actionable problem is narrower:

- **Falling collateral values** are impairing balance sheets of financial intermediaries
- **Solvency vs. liquidity** is the crucial distinction that should drive the response

## The core policy question: liquidity or solvency?

This is the classic diagnosis that determines everything.

**If it's fundamentally a liquidity problem** (institutions are solvent but face runs / can't sell assets fast enough), the central bank playbook applies—Bagehot's rule: *lend freely, against good collateral, at a penalty rate.* The Fed's discount window and emergency facilities exist for exactly this.

**If it's genuinely a solvency problem** (the assets are permanently worth much less because the AI business models are actually impaired), then propping up insolvent firms just socializes losses and creates moral hazard. Bad institutions should be allowed to fail, with orderly resolution to protect depositors and prevent contagion.

The danger is that a solvency problem *presents* as a liquidity problem in the early hours, and political pressure pushes toward blanket bailouts.

## What I'd recommend the government actually do

1. **Central bank as lender of last resort** — provide liquidity to solvent-but-illiquid institutions against collateral, at penalty rates, to stop fire-sale dynamics and runs. This is a legitimate, time-tested role.

2. **Triage, don't bail out indiscriminately** — regulators (Fed, FDIC, Treasury) assess which intermediaries are illiquid vs. actually insolvent. Use existing resolution authority (e.g., Dodd-Frank orderly liquidation) for the insolvent ones.

3. **Protect the financial system's plumbing, not equity holders** — the goal is preventing contagion and protecting depositors/payment systems, not preserving the wealth of AI-company shareholders or the intermediaries that mispriced the collateral.

4. **Communicate clearly** to reduce panic-driven runs, which are self-fulfilling.

5. **Resist doing anything permanent in the crisis window.** Verify the underlying technology claims separately and on a slower timeline. Markets may be overreacting to a hyped announcement.

## What I'd caution against

- **Blanket bailouts** that reward the firms that concentrated risk in a single speculative sector—massive moral hazard.
- **Reactive protectionism / export controls** dressed up as crisis response; those are industrial-policy decisions that shouldn't be made under panic.
- **Suppressing the price signal** itself. If AI equities are genuinely overvalued, a crash is markets working, not markets failing.

## Bottom line

Stabilize the *system* (liquidity, contagion, depositors), let the *market* re-price AI valuations, and let *insolvent* firms fail in an orderly way. The distinction between liquidity support and solvency bailout is the whole game.

I'll note this is a policy-reasoning framework rather than investment or legal advice—and real cases turn heavily on specific facts about which institutions hold what.
