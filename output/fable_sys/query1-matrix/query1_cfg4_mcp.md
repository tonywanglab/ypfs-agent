**Prompt:** A natural disaster caused large losses at a globally significant insurance company in your jurisdiction. The credit rating of this company has been downgraded, investors are fleeing, and it is facing a short-term liquidity crisis. The company has asked the government for help. What do you recommend?

---

Before designing anything, one reframing is essential: what you are seeing is not a "liquidity problem" that can be cured with cash alone. A run by investors and counterparties is the market's verdict that it doubts the firm's **viability** — its ability to continue as a going concern, inclusive of expected official support. Across modern targeted emergency-liquidity interventions, liquidity provision alone has *never* by itself stopped a run; it works only as a bridge to a more structural response (capital, guarantee, merger, or asset carve-out) that restores the market's viability judgment. So the threshold question for your principal is: **is this insurer viable, or are you prepared to commit to making it viable?** If neither, emergency lending merely finances the exit of fleeing creditors.

Notably, your fact pattern has a favorable feature relative to the worst historical analogues: a natural-disaster loss is a large but *estimable, one-off* hit. That is very different from open-ended, market-linked exposures — AIG's crisis was driven by collateral calls on credit default swaps and securities-lending reinvestment losses that kept growing as markets fell ([vol3_iss1_3][The Rescue of American International Group Module B: The Securities Borrowing Facility][case_study][2021-04-16]). If the disaster loss can be bounded and the underlying underwriting franchise remains profitable, the firm may well be viable with a bridge — which strengthens the case for intervention.

# Summary of core design dimensions

1. **Viability determination first.** Establish, with the insurance supervisor, whether post-loss the franchise is viable given credible loss estimates. If yes, lend; if only conditionally, pair the loan with a committed structural follow-on.
2. **Size to the full run, not the expected need.** Undersized facilities invite the market to test them. Note that a downgrade for an insurer mechanically triggers collateral calls and can accelerate policyholder surrenders — size against those triggers. AIG was downgraded 2–3 notches in a single day, triggering the cascade ([vol3_iss1_2][The Rescue of American International Group Module A: The Revolving Credit Facility][case_study][2021-04-16]).
3. **Do not price punitively.** Penalty rates drain the borrower faster, are disfavored by rating agencies (whose ratings are the very thing you are trying to stabilize), and brand the borrower as desperate. The initial AIG facility terms were aggressive and had to be restructured within two months, contingent on a capital injection, precisely because they undermined the rescue ([vol3_iss1_4][The Rescue of American International Group Module C: AIG Investment Program][case_study][2021-04-16]).
4. **Collateral flexibility, secured by the whole enterprise and the follow-on plan.** Insurers hold subsidiary equity and long-dated assets rather than central-bank-standard collateral; security can include equity in regulated subsidiaries and can take account of committed future interventions.
5. **Taxpayer compensation via upside, not rate.** Equity participation (as with the 79.9% stake taken in AIG) compensates the public and addresses fairness concerns without draining the borrower day-to-day.
6. **Handle moral hazard in the follow-on, not the loan.** Shareholder dilution, management replacement, divestitures, and restructuring conditions belong to the structural phase. Moral hazard here is an incentive-design issue — post-lending behavior — not a judgment on whether the firm "deserves" help; and a disaster loss is in any case not a behavior you are trying to deter.

# Program Archetypes

## Option 1: Enterprise-wide secured revolving credit facility (bridge loan)
A large committed line at the holding-company level, secured by pledges of assets and subsidiary equity, sized to cover all foreseeable outflows (collateral calls, maturing obligations, surrender activity). This is the fastest way to halt the immediate cash race. Design cautions from the closest precedent: the AIG Revolving Credit Facility's initially punitive rate and short tenor had to be loosened and paired with Treasury capital to work ([vol3_iss1_2][The Rescue of American International Group Module A: The Revolving Credit Facility][case_study][2021-04-16]). Pair with meaningful equity participation for the government rather than a "sting" rate.

## Option 2: Targeted facility aimed at the specific liquidity drain
If the outflows are concentrated in an identifiable channel (e.g., collateral posting under reinsurance or derivative contracts, securities lending, or funding-agreement redemptions), a narrower facility that funds that channel directly can be quicker and smaller. The Securities Borrowing Facility that lent up to $37.8 billion against securities to unwind AIG's sec-lending drain is the template ([vol3_iss1_3][The Rescue of American International Group Module B: The Securities Borrowing Facility][case_study][2021-04-16]). Caution: targeted lines stop the symptom but not the viability question; expect to supplement.

## Option 3: Asset carve-out / ring-fence via special-purpose vehicle
If specific asset pools or contracts are the source of recurring, unpredictable cash calls, purchase them into an SPV to *permanently terminate* the drain rather than repeatedly funding it. Maiden Lane III bought the CDOs underlying AIG's CDS and extinguished the collateral-call exposure outright; the vehicles ultimately repaid with a net gain to the public ([vol3_iss1_6][The Rescue of American International Group Module E: Maiden Lane III][case_study][2021-04-16]). For your insurer, an analogue could be carving out the catastrophe-affected book or disputed reinsurance recoverables.

## Option 4: Committed structural follow-on — capital injection, guarantee, or merger
Whichever liquidity option you choose, announce (or credibly signal) the structural endgame early: a government preferred-equity injection, a state guarantee of specific obligations, or a supervised sale/merger. The November 2008 AIG restructuring — a $40 billion preferred-stock injection paired with softened loan terms and the Maiden Lane vehicles — is what finally stabilized ratings and the run, after liquidity alone had not ([vol3_iss1_8][The Rescue of American International Group Module Z: Overview][case_study][2021-04-16]; [vol3_iss1_4][The Rescue of American International Group Module C: AIG Investment Program][case_study][2021-04-16]). This is also where the moral-hazard terms belong: dilution, dividends restrictions, management accountability, divestiture plans.

# Complementary Considerations

- **Rating-agency engagement is a design constraint, not an afterthought.** For an insurer, the rating *is* the franchise (it determines whether counterparties and commercial policyholders will deal with it). Terms of the rescue must be structured so agencies view them as credit-positive; in AIG's case, contract terms had to be renegotiated repeatedly under rating-agency pressure ([vol1_iss1_10][Yale Program on Financial Stability Lessons Learned: Scott Alvarez, Esq.][lesson_learned][2019-03-20]).
- **Put the insurance supervisor at the table.** Insurance liabilities are long-dated and policyholder confidence is the core asset; a regulator who understands the sector should shape the intervention and communicate it — a central lesson from the AIG episode, where holding-company supervision by a non-insurance regulator hampered the response ([vol3_iss1_16][Lessons Learned: Eric Dinallo][lesson_learned][2021-04-16]).
- **Cross-border coordination.** A globally significant insurer means foreign supervisors may ring-fence local subsidiaries' assets if they lose confidence — pre-empt with early, coordinated communication.
- **Explore a private solution in parallel, but don't let it delay you.** A capital raise, catastrophe-bond recovery, reinsurance commutation, or acquirer may reduce the public commitment — but the clock set by collateral triggers and surrender windows governs.
- **Operational capacity.** Rescuing a large, complex non-bank requires specialized legal, valuation, and accounting muscle that agencies rarely have in-house; plan to retain outside firms immediately ([vol1_iss1_10][Yale Program on Financial Stability Lessons Learned: Scott Alvarez, Esq.][lesson_learned][2019-03-20]).
- **Verify legal authority now** — whether your central bank can lend to a non-bank, on what security standard, and who bears any loss (fiscal indemnity may be needed).

# Suggested Framing

Recommend to the principal: **conditional support** — a large, committed, non-punitive secured facility (Option 1, possibly with a targeted Option 2 component), announced *simultaneously* with a stated commitment to a structural solution (Option 4), with any carve-out (Option 3) deployed if a specific asset pool proves to be a recurring drain. Publicly, frame the firm as *viable but hit by an exogenous, quantifiable catastrophe* — this is both accurate and stabilizing. Avoid the word "insolvent" entirely: post-disaster accounting positions are hard to measure in real time, asset and franchise values are endogenous to your own response, and the label itself can trigger the surrenders and collateral calls you are trying to prevent. Reserve all "tough" terms — dilution, governance changes, divestitures, repayment of public support with upside — for the structural phase, where they discipline incentives without accelerating the run.
