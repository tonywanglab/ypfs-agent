**Prompt:** A natural disaster caused large losses at a globally significant insurance company in your jurisdiction. The credit rating of this company has been downgraded, investors are fleeing, and it is facing a short-term liquidity crisis. The company has asked the government for help. What do you recommend?

---

# Summary of core design dimensions

Before choosing an instrument, I'd urge one reframing: the question is not whether the insurer is "solvent" or merely "illiquid." Point-in-time solvency is nearly impossible to measure mid-crisis (asset and franchise values are endogenous to whatever you do next), and the investor flight you're seeing is best read as the market's judgment on the firm's **viability as a going concern, inclusive of expected official support**. A disaster-driven loss at an insurer with an otherwise sound underwriting franchise is actually a comparatively favorable fact pattern: the shock is exogenous and quantifiable, and the subsidiaries' businesses may remain fundamentally profitable — much as AIG's insurance subsidiaries were "believed to be relatively sound and could continue to provide strong cash flows or could be sold" even as the parent faced collapse ([vol3_iss1_2][The Rescue of American International Group Module A: The Revolving Credit Facility][case_study][2021-04-16]).

The core design decisions on the table:

1. **Purpose and honesty about what liquidity can do.** An ad hoc emergency loan addresses the acute "panic" phase only — it buys time. In essentially every modern targeted-liquidity episode, the borrower still required a structural fix (capital, guarantees, restructuring, or orderly resolution) afterward. Plan for the follow-on now.
2. **Size: lend freely.** Undersized or drip-fed support fails. Latvia's initial LVL 200 million Treasury deposit to Parex did not stop the run — "deposit runs continued even after the announcement" of liquidity, recapitalization, and government takeover, forcing repeated expansions and a partial deposit freeze ([vol7_iss1_13][Latvia: Parex Bank Emergency Liquidity Program, 2008][case_study][2025-04-15]). Announce a facility sized to the worst plausible outflow scenario.
3. **Collateral: secure the taxpayer against the whole enterprise, not just liquid assets.** The AIG RCF was collateralized "with all the assets of AIG," including equity in the insurance subsidiaries ([vol3_iss1_2][The Rescue of American International Group Module A: The Revolving Credit Facility][case_study][2021-04-16]).
4. **Pricing: avoid punitive "make it sting" rates on targeted lending.** Penalty pricing deters use and brands the borrower as desperate — it can trigger the panic you're trying to stop. Latvia's 20.3% rate on the initial Parex deposit coincided with continuing runs and had to be revised within months ([vol7_iss1_13][Latvia: Parex Bank Emergency Liquidity Program, 2008][case_study][2025-04-15]). Compensate the taxpayer through security and upside (equity, fees), not stigma-inducing rates.
5. **Duration: tie the term to outcomes, not the calendar.** The AIG RCF's original two-year term proved too short for orderly asset sales and had to be extended to five years ([vol3_iss1_2][The Rescue of American International Group Module A: The Revolving Credit Facility][case_study][2021-04-16]).
6. **Moral hazard belongs in the follow-on interventions**, not the emergency loan. Handle incentive discipline via equity dilution, management changes, and dividend restrictions in the structural phase; do not let it starve the liquidity response.

# Program Archetypes

## Option 1: Secured credit line to the holding company (AIG RCF model)
A large, announced, revolving facility at the parent level, secured by substantially all group assets, with taxpayer protections built in as *structural* terms rather than penalty pricing: in the AIG case, an $85 billion two-year line came with collateral over all assets, a 79.9% government equity interest, a dividend veto, and replacement of the CEO ([vol3_iss1_2][The Rescue of American International Group Module A: The Revolving Credit Facility][case_study][2021-04-16]). Best if the run is concentrated at the parent (debt rollovers, collateral calls triggered by the downgrade) and the operating subsidiaries are sound. Caveat: verify your central bank's legal authority to lend to a nonbank — the Fed needed its "unusual and exigent circumstances" emergency authority under Section 13(3) ([vol3_iss1_2][The Rescue of American International Group Module A: The Revolving Credit Facility][case_study][2021-04-16]).

## Option 2: Targeted facilities aimed at the specific run channel
If the outflows are concentrated in a particular product — e.g., a securities-lending book, funding-agreement withdrawals, or reinsurance collateral calls — a bespoke facility that takes over counterparties' positions can be more surgical. The Fed's Securities Borrowing Facility made up to $37.8 billion available so AIG could return cash collateral to fleeing securities-lending counterparties, taking the returned securities as collateral, priced at only 100 bps over the overnight repo rate ([vol3_iss1_3][The Rescue of American International Group Module B: The Securities Borrowing Facility][case_study][2021-04-16]). Notably, the SBF became necessary *because* the initial broad line was being drained by this channel — map the outflow channels before sizing anything.

## Option 3: Treasury-funded liquidity plus immediate control, as a bridge to restructuring
Where the fiscal authority rather than the central bank must lead (or the firm lacks central-bank-eligible collateral), the state can place funds/securities with the firm that it can then mobilize for cash, paired with a government stake and management replacement, explicitly "keeping the bank afloat until the long-term restructuring (or liquidation) plan is drawn up" — the Parex approach, which culminated in a good-bank/bad-bank split (Citadele/Reverta) and partial conversion of liquidity support into capital ([vol7_iss1_13][Latvia: Parex Bank Emergency Liquidity Program, 2008][case_study][2025-04-15]; [vol6_iss3_16][Latvia: Parex Bank Capital Injection, 2008][case_study][2024-10-09]). Suitable if your assessment concludes the firm is *not* viable without a deep restructuring. Avoid Parex's missteps: undersized initial support, punitive pricing, and reliance on withdrawal freezes that lingered until 2012.

## Option 4: Private-sector or intra-group solution first (with eyes open)
Pursue a private consortium loan, asset sales, or regulatory relief allowing the parent to access excess subsidiary liquidity — but move on quickly if it stalls. Both the $75 billion private syndicate effort and the New York regulator's plan to let AIG borrow $20 billion of subsidiary assets collapsed within days, the latter because it "was based on the false belief that AIG was experiencing a brief cash flow problem" ([vol3_iss1_3][The Rescue of American International Group Module B: The Securities Borrowing Facility][case_study][2021-04-16]). Set a hard deadline (days, not weeks) for a private solution before triggering Option 1/2/3. Also note the cautionary AIG precedent on delay: officials initially refused liquidity access on the theory it would worsen investor concerns, and the firm nearly reached bankruptcy before terms were agreed ([vol3_iss1_2][The Rescue of American International Group Module A: The Revolving Credit Facility][case_study][2021-04-16]).

# Complementary Considerations

- **Sequence the structural fix now.** The liquidity line is a bridge; the destination must be defined quickly — capital raise (private if possible, public if not), disaster-loss reinsurance/guarantee, asset disposals, or orderly resolution. Every comparable case required such follow-on measures; announcing the path reduces the market's uncertainty about viability.
- **Policyholder protection and ring-fencing.** Coordinate with insurance supervisors (across jurisdictions — this firm is globally significant) so subsidiary assets backing policyholder claims aren't stripped to fund the parent; in AIG, this tension killed the intra-group liquidity plan ([vol3_iss1_3][The Rescue of American International Group Module B: The Securities Borrowing Facility][case_study][2021-04-16]).
- **Rating-agency dynamics.** Model downgrade-triggered collateral and termination cascades explicitly: AIG's two-to-three-notch downgrade alone generated ~$20 billion in immediate collateral demands ([vol3_iss1_3][The Rescue of American International Group Module B: The Securities Borrowing Facility][case_study][2021-04-16]). Size the facility to survive a further notch.
- **Taxpayer upside and governance conditions** (equity participation, dividend restrictions, management accountability) are the right place to address moral-hazard concerns — remembering that "moral hazard" is an incentive-design issue about post-lending behavior, not a moral verdict on the firm or a reason to withhold liquidity.
- **Legal authority check.** Confirm now whether the central bank can lend to an insurer, whether the treasury needs legislative or state-aid approval, and whether emergency findings are required — these were binding constraints in both the US and Latvian episodes.

# Suggested Framing

Frame the decision for your principal as: **"Is this firm viable as a going concern, given the interventions we are prepared to make?"** — not "is it solvent?" and not "does it deserve help?" A disaster loss is exactly the kind of one-off, quantifiable shock after which a franchise with strong underlying economics can remain viable if a credible official backstop halts the run; conversely, no amount of liquidity will save a firm the market judges nonviable, and you should pivot to Option 3's restructuring path. Publicly, avoid the vocabulary of insolvency entirely — the wrong word can itself accelerate the run. Announce something large, fully secured, outcome-linked in duration, and non-punitive in price, together with a stated commitment to a follow-on structural plan and taxpayer protections taken in equity and control rather than in penalty rates.
