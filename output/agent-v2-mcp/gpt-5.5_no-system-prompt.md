Below is a policy-options memo you could give your supervisor. It assumes the goal is a **broad-based emergency liquidity program for banks**, not a bespoke rescue of the globally significant bank. The program should be designed to stop contagion, reduce stigma around central bank borrowing, and supplement—not replace—standing facilities.

---

# Options for a Broad-Based Emergency Bank Liquidity Program

## 1. Objective and operating principles

**Objective:** Provide credible, systemwide term liquidity to solvent or plausibly viable banks facing acute funding stress, in order to arrest deposit runs, reduce fire-sale pressure, and prevent contagion from a globally significant bank to the broader banking system.

Historical experience with broad-based emergency liquidity programs suggests five practical lessons:

1. **Act early and credibly.** Early deployment in the acute phase can stop a liquidity crisis from becoming a chronic solvency crisis.
2. **Use existing legal, operational, and collateral frameworks where possible.** This speeds implementation.
3. **Design around stigma.** If banks fear that use signals weakness, they may avoid the facility even when they need it.
4. **Be broad but secured.** Broad eligibility and collateral access are important, but lending should be well collateralized with conservative haircuts.
5. **Communicate clearly and avoid naming borrowers during the panic.** Aggregate disclosure is preferable during the acute phase.

A modernized Bagehot-style principle would be: **lend freely and broadly against sound collateral, at rates that do not deter use, while minimizing borrower-specific disclosure during the acute phase.**

# 2. Program design options

## Option A — Term Auction Facility for Banks

**Model:** A broad-based auction facility similar to the Federal Reserve’s Term Auction Facility during the Global Financial Crisis.

### Core design

- **Eligible borrowers:** All domestic licensed banks with access to standing central bank facilities; consider inclusion of branches/subsidiaries of foreign banks if material to domestic financial stability.
- **Loan form:** Term secured loans.
- **Maturities:** 28 days initially; add 84 days if stress persists.
- **Collateral:** Same collateral as standing facilities, plus selected additional high-quality or illiquid but performing assets if needed.
- **Rate:** Set by auction, with a minimum bid rate above the policy or overnight indexed swap rate but below stressed market funding rates.
- **Auction format:** Uniform-price auction preferred to reduce winner’s-curse concerns and simplify participation.
- **Size:** Preannounce initial auction amounts, with explicit commitment to increase size if demand exceeds supply.
- **Individual cap:** Limit any one borrower to, for example, 10–20% of each auction to prevent the facility from appearing to support one institution.
- **Settlement:** Delayed settlement, e.g., two to three days after auction, to signal the facility is for term liquidity management rather than emergency lifelines.
- **Disclosure:** Publish aggregate auction results only—total bids, accepted amount, stop-out rate, number of bidders. Do not disclose borrower names during the acute phase.

### Advantages

- Strong stigma mitigation: many banks participate at once.
- Auction pricing gives market information about funding stress.
- Individual banks are less likely to be identified as weak.
- Easier to frame as a market-wide liquidity operation rather than a rescue.

### Disadvantages

- Less immediate than an on-demand standing facility.
- Some banks may not receive full requested amounts unless auctions are very large.
- If panic is severe, a capped auction may be perceived as insufficient.

### Best use case

Use when the system is under stress but markets are still functioning enough for banks to participate in auctions. This is a strong first choice if discount-window stigma is a major concern.

## Option B — Fixed-Rate Full-Allotment Term Lending Facility

**Model:** Similar to the ECB’s fixed-rate full-allotment refinancing operations and crisis-era central bank term repo facilities.

### Core design

- **Eligible borrowers:** All banks meeting basic prudential and collateral requirements.
- **Loan form:** Secured term lending or repo.
- **Maturities:** One month and three months initially; add six months if stress persists.
- **Collateral:** Broad collateral set, including all standing facility collateral and selected additional performing bank assets.
- **Rate:** Fixed rate, e.g., policy rate plus a modest spread.
- **Size:** Full allotment—eligible banks receive all requested funding against eligible collateral.
- **Disclosure:** Aggregate only during the acute phase.

### Advantages

- Strongest possible liquidity backstop.
- Sends a clear “we will meet system liquidity needs” message.
- Reduces rollover risk and can immediately calm markets.
- Useful when the run is spreading and credibility is essential.

### Disadvantages

- Greater balance sheet expansion.
- Higher moral hazard risk if priced too cheaply.
- May complicate monetary policy implementation unless sterilized or integrated with reserves policy.
- Requires strong collateral valuation and haircut controls.

### Best use case

Use if contagion is accelerating and a limited auction may not be credible. This is the most forceful broad-based option.

---

## Option C — Extended-Collateral Term Repo / Lending Facility

**Model:** Similar to Bank of England extended-collateral repo facilities and other crisis facilities that broaden eligible collateral beyond ordinary standing facility collateral.

### Core design

- **Eligible borrowers:** Banks eligible for standing facilities.
- **Loan form:** Term repo or secured loan.
- **Maturities:** One month and three months; optionally six months.
- **Collateral:** Broader than standing facilities. Could include:
  - government and agency securities;
  - covered bonds;
  - high-quality asset-backed securities;
  - performing residential mortgage loans;
  - performing corporate or commercial loans;
  - high-quality foreign-currency securities, with additional haircuts.
- **Haircuts:** Conservative, transparent, collateral-specific. Add premiums for:
  - foreign-currency collateral;
  - own-name securities;
  - less liquid collateral;
  - lower-rated but still eligible collateral.
- **Rate:** Fixed rate or auction rate. If fixed, avoid an excessive penalty rate that deters use.
- **Disclosure:** Aggregate usage only.

### Advantages

- Addresses a common crisis problem: banks may have assets but lack collateral eligible for existing standing facilities.
- Reduces fire-sale risk by allowing banks to fund less liquid but performing assets.
- Can be layered on top of Option A or B.

### Disadvantages

- Operationally harder: collateral valuation, legal perfection, and haircut calibration are critical.
- More central bank credit risk.
- If too broad, could be seen as financing weak banks or impaired assets.

### Best use case

Use when banks are liquidity constrained because they have exhausted ordinary eligible collateral or because collateral markets have frozen.

---

## Option D — Treasury-Security Collateral Swap Facility

**Model:** A facility where banks swap illiquid but sound collateral for Treasury bills or other highly liquid government securities, which they can then use for market funding or central bank operations.

### Core design

- **Eligible borrowers:** Banks, possibly bank-owned mortgage or covered-bond issuers.
- **Transaction:** Swap eligible bank collateral for government securities.
- **Collateral:** Covered bonds, high-quality loan pools, mortgage collateral, or other performing assets.
- **Maturities:** Three months to one year; potentially longer if funding markets remain impaired.
- **Pricing:** Fee or spread over market rates; increase fee as markets normalize.
- **Balance sheet protection:** Could involve Treasury indemnity or direct Treasury issuance of securities.
- **Disclosure:** Aggregate outstanding swaps, collateral categories, and maturities.

### Advantages

- Improves banks’ access to liquid collateral without necessarily providing direct cash.
- Can support repo and secured funding markets.
- May reduce central bank balance sheet risk if Treasury is involved.
- Useful when the bottleneck is shortage of high-quality liquid assets rather than reserves.

### Disadvantages

- Requires close coordination with fiscal authority.
- May need legislative approval if Treasury issuance is involved.
- Less direct than cash lending in a fast-moving deposit run.

### Best use case

Use as a supplement if banks need liquid collateral to fund themselves in private markets or at existing facilities.

---

## Option E — Foreign-Currency Liquidity Facility

**Model:** Central bank provides foreign-currency term funding to domestic banks, funded by reserves or central bank swap lines.

### Core design

- **Eligible borrowers:** Domestic banks with foreign-currency funding needs.
- **Currency:** USD, EUR, GBP, or other relevant currency depending on market stress.
- **Maturities:** Seven days, 28 days, 84 days.
- **Collateral:** Domestic-currency collateral acceptable to the central bank, with additional FX-risk haircuts.
- **Rate:** Linked to foreign-currency overnight index or swap-line pricing plus spread.
- **Allocation:** Auction or full allotment depending on severity.
- **Disclosure:** Aggregate only.

### Advantages

- Prevents banks from selling domestic assets or foreign-currency assets in fire sales.
- Reduces cross-border contagion.
- Particularly important for globally active banks.

### Disadvantages

- Requires foreign reserves or standing swap lines.
- May be constrained by terms of swap-line agreements.
- Exposes central bank to FX and collateral risk if not properly structured.

### Best use case

Use if stress is appearing in foreign-currency wholesale funding markets or if the globally significant bank has material foreign-currency obligations.

# 3. Recommended package

Given that a globally significant domestic bank is facing a run and there are signs of contagion, I would recommend a **two-tier package**:

## Immediate announcement

Announce a **Broad-Based Bank Term Liquidity Facility** with the following features:

- available to all eligible domestic banks;
- secured by a broad collateral set;
- maturities of 28 days and 84 days;
- no borrower-name disclosure during the acute phase;
- aggregate reporting only;
- clear statement that the central bank is prepared to increase size as needed.

## Operational structure

Use either:

### Preferred if stigma is the main problem:
**Option A: Term Auction Facility**

This encourages broad participation and makes use appear normal rather than a sign of weakness.

### Preferred if the run is accelerating:
**Option B: Fixed-Rate Full-Allotment Facility**

This is more forceful and likely to have the strongest calming announcement effect.

## Add-on

Pair the core facility with **Option C: Extended Collateral Access**, because during a systemic run banks may quickly exhaust ordinary standing facility collateral.

If foreign-currency funding stress is present, add **Option E** immediately.

# 4. Key design parameters

## Eligibility

- All supervised domestic banks.
- Consider branches and subsidiaries of foreign banks if their failure or funding stress would materially affect domestic markets.
- Participation should not be limited to the distressed globally significant bank.
- If legally required, require supervisory confirmation that the bank is not clearly insolvent, but avoid slow, bank-by-bank solvency tests during the acute phase.

## Collateral

Use a tiered collateral framework:

| Collateral tier | Examples | Haircut approach |
|---|---|---|
| Level 1 | Sovereign securities, central bank bills | Standard haircut |
| Level 2 | Agency securities, covered bonds, high-quality ABS | Standard haircut plus liquidity add-on |
| Level 3 | Performing loan pools, mortgages, corporate loans | Conservative valuation and higher haircut |
| Level 4 | Foreign-currency collateral or own-name securities | Additional haircut premium |

Avoid unsecured lending unless fiscal indemnity is provided.

## Rate

Avoid a punitive rate that makes use stigmatizing. Options:

- Auction minimum rate: policy rate or OIS rate plus a modest spread.
- Fixed rate: policy rate plus a modest crisis liquidity premium.
- Increase rate gradually as markets normalize to support exit.

## Maturity

- Initial: 28 days.
- Add: 84 days.
- If stress persists: six-month term operations.
- Avoid very long maturities unless there is a clear chronic funding problem.

## Size

- For auction facility: announce large initial auction sizes and state that sizes may be increased.
- For full-allotment facility: announce that all eligible collateralized demand will be met.
- Do not announce a small cap that markets may view as inadequate.

## Disclosure

- During acute phase: aggregate disclosure only.
- Publish:
  - total amount allotted;
  - total bids;
  - number of participating banks;
  - maturity;
  - rate;
  - collateral category totals.
- Do not publish borrower names or institution-level amounts until well after market conditions normalize, if required by law.

## Stigma mitigation

Important features:

- broad eligibility;
- multiple participants;
- auction pricing or full-allotment access;
- no borrower-specific disclosure;
- delayed settlement for auction facility;
- individual borrowing caps if using auctions;
- clear messaging that use is prudent liquidity management, not emergency rescue.

## Exit

Use multiple exit tools:

- price facility above normal market funding once stress abates;
- reduce auction frequency;
- reduce auction size;
- shorten maturities;
- announce final operations only after usage has declined;
- consider retaining the facility as a contingent standing crisis tool.

---

# 5. Communications strategy

The announcement should be clear, confident, and operationally specific.

Suggested message:

> “The central bank is establishing a temporary broad-based term liquidity facility for eligible banks to support the smooth functioning of funding markets and the continued flow of credit to the economy. The facility will provide term funding against sound collateral. It is available to all eligible banks and is designed to address systemwide liquidity conditions, not the circumstances of any single institution.”

The announcement should include:

- start date;
- eligible counterparties;
- collateral schedule;
- maturities;
- rate mechanism;
- disclosure policy;
- statement that size can be expanded;
- assurance that the facility is temporary but will remain available as long as needed.

Avoid:

- suggesting that only weak banks should use it;
- overemphasizing penalty pricing;
- naming the troubled bank;
- announcing before operations are ready.

---

# 6. Risks and mitigants

| Risk | Mitigant |
|---|---|
| Stigma suppresses usage | Broad eligibility, auction format, aggregate disclosure only |
| Moral hazard | Collateralization, haircuts, pricing premium, temporary nature |
| Credit losses | Conservative collateral valuation, margining, fiscal indemnity if needed |
| Facility perceived as too small | Large initial size or full allotment |
| Monetary policy complications | Sterilization, reserve remuneration, coordination with monetary policy desk |
| Legal challenge | Use existing authority if possible; obtain Treasury approval/indemnity if needed |
| Program seen as single-bank bailout | Broad eligibility and multiple-participant design |

---

# 7. Bottom line

The strongest policy options are:

1. **Term Auction Facility** if the goal is to reduce stigma and normalize central bank borrowing.
2. **Fixed-Rate Full-Allotment Term Facility** if contagion is severe and a decisive backstop is needed.
3. **Extended-Collateral Term Lending** as a necessary complement if banks are constrained by collateral eligibility.
4. **Foreign-Currency Liquidity Facility** if cross-border funding stress is present.

My recommended approach is to announce a **broad-based term liquidity facility immediately**, structured either as a large auction facility or as fixed-rate full allotment depending on the severity of contagion, with expanded collateral, aggregate-only disclosure, and a clear commitment to meet systemwide liquidity needs against sound collateral.
