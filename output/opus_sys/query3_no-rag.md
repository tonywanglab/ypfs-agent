# Analyzing the Crisis Response

This scenario blends multiple distinct problems that require *different* tools. The key is separating them, because the biggest policy error would be treating them as one thing.

## First, distinguish the three separate issues

1. **A technological/geopolitical development** (Chinese AI advance)
2. **A repricing of AI equities** (markets adjusting valuations)
3. **A potential financial-system liquidity crisis** (the actual government concern)

Only the third is clearly a legitimate emergency policy target. Let me address each.

## The equity crash itself: mostly leave it alone

A stock crash driven by genuinely new information is markets doing their job. If AI companies are worth less because a competitor undermined their moat, propping up their prices would:
- Reward mispriced bets and encourage future recklessness (moral hazard)
- Misallocate capital toward firms the market now judges less viable
- Put taxpayers behind speculative valuations

**The government should not bail out equity holders or AI companies as such.** Losses on speculative tech investments should fall on those who took the risk.

## The financial stability problem: this is the real job

The classic distinction here (Bagehot's rule) is between **illiquidity** and **insolvency**:

- **Illiquidity**: fundamentally sound institutions can't meet short-term obligations because assets are frozen or fire-selling. → Central bank should **lend freely, against good collateral, at a penalty rate.**
- **Insolvency**: institutions whose liabilities genuinely exceed assets. → These should be resolved, not rescued; wind them down, protect insured depositors, impose losses on shareholders and creditors.

**Concrete steps for the Federal Reserve / Treasury:**
- Provide emergency liquidity facilities to solvent intermediaries against sound collateral
- Communicate clearly and quickly to prevent panic-driven runs (much of a liquidity crisis is expectations)
- Coordinate with the FDIC/OCC on assessing which institutions are truly insolvent vs. merely illiquid
- Ring-fence contagion so problems at over-exposed lenders don't take down the payments system

## The harder question buried in the scenario

The alarming detail is that **loan collateral was heavily AI equity**. That reveals a systemic concentration risk—financial intermediaries treating a speculative sector as safe backing. This is a regulatory/supervisory failure worth examining *after* the acute phase:
- Were leverage and collateral-quality rules too lax?
- Was there hidden interconnection among lenders exposed to the same sector?

## What to be skeptical of

- Pressure to "restore confidence in AI" by subsidizing the sector—this conflates industrial policy with financial stability.
- Blanket guarantees that protect everyone, which maximize moral hazard.
- Overreacting to the *geopolitical* angle by rushing protectionist or subsidy measures under crisis cover, before the technology's real significance is even verified.

## Bottom line

Provide **liquidity to the solvent, resolution for the insolvent, and no rescue for equity holders**. Let the AI repricing happen; contain only the financial contagion. Then investigate why the banking system had such concentrated exposure to a single volatile sector.
