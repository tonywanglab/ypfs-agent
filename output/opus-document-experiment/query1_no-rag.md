# Assessing the Situation: A Systemic Insurer in Distress

This is a serious policy question with echoes of the 2008 AIG crisis. Here's how I'd approach the recommendation.

## First, Establish the Facts

Before recommending anything, I'd want answers to critical questions:

- **Is it solvency or liquidity?** A liquidity crunch (can't access cash quickly) is very different from insolvency (liabilities exceed assets). The former may be temporary and fixable; the latter is deeper.
- **How systemic is it really?** What are its interconnections—counterparties, reinsurance chains, policyholders, funding markets? Would its failure cascade?
- **What caused this?** A genuine natural disaster (bad luck) differs from underlying mismanagement or excessive risk-taking exposed by the disaster.
- **What's the scale?** How large is the gap, and over what timeframe?

## Guiding Principles

I would resist a reflexive bailout while also resisting reflexive refusal. Key principles:

1. **Protect the system, not the shareholders.** Any intervention should aim to prevent broader harm to policyholders and financial stability—not to rescue investors or management from their losses.

2. **Avoid moral hazard.** Bailing out firms teaches them that risk is privatized in good times and socialized in bad times. Terms must be punitive enough to deter future recklessness.

3. **Taxpayers should be protected and compensated** for any risk they take on.

## My Likely Recommendation (a tiered approach)

**If it's a liquidity problem in a solvent firm:**
- Provide a **secured, penalty-rate loan** (à la a lender of last resort), fully collateralized, with strict conditions.
- Attach conditions: senior management changes, dividend/bonus suspension, and possibly equity warrants so the public shares in any upside.

**If it's deeper insolvency:**
- Do **not** simply write a check. Consider an **orderly resolution**: government facilitates a private-sector acquisition, an assisted merger, or a structured wind-down that protects policyholders while imposing losses on shareholders and (where appropriate) creditors.
- Protect policyholders through guarantee schemes rather than propping up the failed entity.

**In all cases:**
- Insist that **shareholders and management bear losses first.**
- Ensure **transparency** and legislative/regulatory oversight of any public funds.
- Coordinate internationally, given the firm's global significance.

## Longer-Term Follow-Up

- Review why a "globally significant" insurer had inadequate capital and liquidity buffers against foreseeable catastrophe risk.
- Strengthen macroprudential regulation, stress testing, and resolution planning ("living wills") for systemic insurers.

## Bottom Line

I would recommend **conditional, secured support only if failure poses genuine systemic risk**—structured to protect policyholders and the financial system while forcing losses onto those who took the risks. The goal is stability without rewarding failure.
