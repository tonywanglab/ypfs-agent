**Prompt:** A natural disaster caused large losses at a globally significant insurance company in your jurisdiction. The credit rating of this company has been downgraded, investors are fleeing, and it is facing a short-term liquidity crisis. The company has asked the government for help. What do you recommend?

# Assessment and Recommendations

This is a serious situation, but before recommending any government action, I'd need to work through several critical questions. Let me lay out a structured approach.

## First: Diagnose the Problem Precisely

The key distinction is **liquidity vs. solvency**:

- **Liquidity crisis**: The company is fundamentally sound—assets exceed liabilities—but can't convert assets to cash fast enough to meet immediate obligations. This is potentially fixable.
- **Solvency crisis**: The disaster losses have genuinely destroyed the company's capital base, and it owes more than it's worth. This is a different problem entirely.

I would not commit public funds until this is clearly established, likely through an emergency review by the insurance regulator.

## Second: Assess Systemic Risk

"Globally significant" is the crucial phrase. I'd want to understand:

- **Interconnectedness**: Who are its counterparties? Does failure cascade to banks, pension funds, or other insurers?
- **Policyholder impact**: How many people/businesses lose coverage or claims if it fails?
- **Substitutability**: Can other insurers absorb its book of business?
- **Contagion risk**: Will its failure trigger panic across the sector?

The stronger the systemic threat, the more justified some intervention becomes.

## Recommended Approach

**If it's a liquidity problem with systemic risk:**
- Provide *temporary, secured, penalty-rate* liquidity support (lender-of-last-resort style)—not a gift. This follows the classic Bagehot principle: lend freely against good collateral at high rates.
- Attach strict conditions: management accountability, no dividends/bonuses, oversight.

**If it's a solvency problem:**
- Do **not** simply bail out shareholders and management. That creates moral hazard and is politically and ethically indefensible.
- Consider resolution tools: orderly wind-down, forced sale, bridge institution, or ensuring policyholder protection schemes/guarantee funds activate.
- Shareholders and creditors should bear losses first ("bail-in" before bailout).

## Key Principles I'd Insist On

1. **Protect policyholders and the public, not investors.** The public interest is in continuity of coverage and financial stability—not preserving equity value.
2. **Minimize moral hazard.** If firms expect rescue, they take excessive risk. Any support must sting.
3. **Taxpayer protection.** Any funds should be recoverable, collateralized, and come with upside (e.g., equity stakes/warrants).
4. **Transparency and legal authority.** Act within the resolution framework, not through ad hoc discretion.

## What I'd Recommend Immediately

1. Convene the regulator, central bank, and treasury.
2. Order an emergency solvency assessment.
3. Explore private-sector solutions first (capital raise, acquisition).
4. Prepare a resolution plan as a backstop.
5. Only deploy public support if systemic risk is real, and only on hard terms.

**Bottom line:** Stabilize the system and protect policyholders—but don't rescue the company's owners from the consequences of the risk they bore. The question is not "how do we save this company?" but "how do we prevent harm to the public and financial system at least cost and least moral hazard?"
