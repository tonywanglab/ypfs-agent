**Prompt:** A Chinese company has just announced that it has achieved recursive self-improvement of a frontier LLM, without using Nvidia chips. This causes the equity prices of U.S. AI companies to crash, and the collateral used to back AI-company loans to fall significantly. This causes solvency concerns at several financial intermediaries, with a liquidity crisis feared to come. What should the government do?

---

# Assessing the Scenario

Before recommending action, it's worth separating the distinct problems here, because they call for different responses—and some of them may not warrant government action at all.

## Distinguishing the layers

**1. The AI capability claim itself.** A single company's announcement, especially one that's hard to independently verify, shouldn't drive policy. "Recursive self-improvement" is an extraordinary claim requiring extraordinary evidence. The government's first move on *this* dimension is verification and intelligence assessment, not market intervention. Markets frequently overreact to announcements that don't pan out.

**2. The equity repricing.** If US AI equities crash because a competitor has genuinely eroded their moat, that's the market doing its job—reallocating capital based on new information. **Falling stock prices are not, by themselves, something the government should cushion.** Backstopping equity holders would be both unjust (bailing out speculative bets) and create moral hazard.

**3. The financial stability problem.** This is where legitimate government concern lies. If AI-company equity/debt was used as *collateral* throughout the financial system, a repricing can trigger fire sales, margin calls, and contagion that's disproportionate to the underlying economic news. This is a classic mechanism where a solvency shock becomes a *liquidity* crisis.

# What the government should actually do

## The Fed's role (liquidity, not solvency)
The central bank's job in a liquidity crisis is well-established (Bagehot's dictum): **lend freely, against good collateral, at a penalty rate.**

- Provide liquidity to *solvent* institutions facing runs, so they aren't forced into fire sales that spread the panic
- Distinguish carefully between institutions that are illiquid (temporarily can't sell assets at fair value) versus insolvent (genuinely underwater). The former get support; the latter should be resolved.

## The Treasury/regulators' role
- **Assess actual exposures.** How concentrated is AI collateral? Which intermediaries, and how interconnected? This determines whether it's a contained problem or systemic.
- **Resolve failing institutions in an orderly way** rather than bailing out shareholders. Wipe out equity, protect the payment system and insured depositors.
- **Avoid rescuing the AI companies or their investors.** Their business-model risk is not a public liability.

## What to avoid
- Don't prop up equity prices or intervene in the underlying tech competition through financial policy.
- Don't create the expectation that any concentrated bet gets a backstop.
- Don't conflate "protecting the financial system's plumbing" with "protecting the people who lost money."

# The deeper lesson (separate from crisis response)
The scenario reveals a structural vulnerability worth addressing *after* the fire is out: **AI-linked assets became systemically important collateral.** That's a regulatory question about leverage, collateral quality, and concentration—analogous to how mortgage-backed securities became load-bearing in 2008. The right long-run response is prudential (capital requirements, haircuts on volatile collateral), not perpetual crisis backstops.

---

**Bottom line:** Backstop the *plumbing* (liquidity to solvent institutions to prevent contagion), let the *bets* fail (equity holders and insolvent firms), verify the *claim* independently, and treat the collateral-concentration problem as a regulatory reform once stability returns.

One honest caveat: the line between "illiquid" and "insolvent" is genuinely hard to draw in real time, and getting it wrong in either direction is costly—which is exactly why these decisions are so contested in practice.
