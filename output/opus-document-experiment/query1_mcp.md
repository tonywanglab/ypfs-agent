# Recommendation: Responding to a Distressed Globally Significant Insurer

## Executive summary

The situation you describe—a large-loss event, a rating downgrade, fleeing investors, and an acute short-term liquidity crisis at a globally significant insurer—maps almost exactly onto the 2008 rescue of American International Group (AIG), the most instructive precedent in the corpus. The central lesson is this: **do not treat the liquidity crisis as the problem to be solved. Treat it as a symptom of a viability question, and answer that question first.** Emergency liquidity alone has never, in the surveyed cases, "cured" a run.

Below I set out a recommended sequence, grounded in the corpus.

---

## 1. First, diagnose viability—not just illiquidity

The Yale survey on *Ad Hoc Emergency Liquidity (AHEL) Programs* is emphatic on this point:

> "The liquidity crisis should instead be viewed as the manifestation of the market's assessing the firm as nonviable as a going concern… authorities should provide AHEL assistance only to institutions that they have deemed viable or that they have committed to make viable through additional interventions."

Crucially, the surveyed cases show that even when authorities sized liquidity to cover *all* possible short-term outflows, **"in no cases did liquidity provision alone prove a 'cure' to the run."** So before committing public resources, you must determine whether this insurer is (a) fundamentally viable but illiquid, or (b) facing a solvency/viability problem that liquidity cannot fix. The distinction between literal accounting solvency and *viability as a going concern* (inclusive of the effect of planned interventions) is the right lens.

**Before anything else:** get your people inside the firm. In AIG, the authorities were "learning the company as [they] were executing the rescue" because the central bank was not its regulator—a serious handicap. You should immediately deploy a team to understand the firm's structure, the true size of its liquidity need, and the collateral available.

## 2. Pursue a private-sector solution first

In AIG, the authorities' first instinct—correctly—was to convene major financial institutions to assemble a private bridge loan, and to explore a forced merger with a healthier institution. A private solution (including acquisition by a stronger firm) avoids taxpayer exposure and moral hazard. **Exhaust this before public funds.** Only when the private consortium collapsed did the government face what officials called a "binary choice" between disorderly bankruptcy and public support.

## 3. Understand *why* the downgrade is so dangerous—and protect the rating

The rating downgrade is not a side issue; it is the engine of the crisis. In AIG, downgrades triggered massive collateral calls and contract terminations. Two related lessons follow:

- **Beware self-inflicted downgrades.** The AIG lesson from Jim Millstein is direct: haircuts or distressed-exchange demands on counterparties can push a rating agency to assign a default rating, "which can result in massive collateral calls and potential illiquidity of the already weak firm." Well-intentioned "burden-sharing" can backfire and *increase* the government's total spend. "A financial institution cannot operate without at least an investment-grade rating."
- **Terms of assistance must be evaluated through the rating agencies' eyes.** In AIG, the initial rescue loan's onerous terms *worsened* the firm's leverage ratios and drew rating-agency concern—precisely the opposite of the intended stabilizing effect.

## 4. If you must provide liquidity, design it as a *bridge*, not a cure—and resist punitive terms

If viability is (or can be made) sound, emergency liquidity is appropriate—but only as a bridge to structural measures. The corpus overturns the classic "penalty rate" element of Bagehot's dictum for single-firm rescues:

> "A penalty rate in such a case is liable to instead accelerate the drain on the affected institution's financial resources (and be disfavored by the market and the rating agencies)—thus intensifying crisis-fighters' race against the clock."

The AIG Revolving Credit Facility charged LIBOR + 850bps to limit moral hazard; the firm's position kept deteriorating and the rate had to be cut by 550bps weeks later. Similarly, Latvia's Parex rate had to be walked back. **Lesson: "lend freely" survives; "penalty rate" largely does not** for AHEL. Do not try to solve moral hazard through liquidity terms—address it later, in the structural response.

On collateral, apply flexibility: if a merger or capital injection is coming, you can consider the acquirer's balance sheet or incoming capital when assessing repayment.

## 5. Follow the bridge with structural measures

Liquidity buys time; it does not fix the balance sheet. In AIG the structural responses included:

- **Capital injection** (equity) to reduce leverage and reassure rating agencies;
- **Asset purchases / special-purpose vehicles** to remove the specific loss-generating exposures and *stop the collateral calls at their source* (the true liquidity drain);
- **Orderly asset sales** over time to repay the government.

The *Survey of Ad Hoc Capital Injections* reinforces that in the acute phase, **financial-stability concerns should outweigh moral-hazard concerns**; punitive ex-ante conditions are better replaced by tough *ex-post* measures (management discipline; bail-in where a clear, credible legal basis exists).

## 6. Protect taxpayers and manage moral hazard the right way

Rather than punitive rates, AIG protected taxpayers through:
- **Senior secured, collateralized lending;**
- **An equity stake (the "equity kicker")** giving taxpayers upside if the firm recovered (which ultimately produced a ~$22.7bn profit);
- **Governance rights** (dividend vetoes, board influence, management change).

Note the caution: the equity kicker/effective nationalization drew litigation and "nationalization" criticism. Keep any equity stake below the consolidation threshold (AIG used 79.9%) if you wish to avoid consolidating the firm's liabilities onto the public balance sheet, and ensure the legal structure can withstand challenge.

## 7. Confirm your legal authority and coordinate

The corpus repeatedly stresses that many jurisdictions in 2008 lacked a **resolution regime** for large non-bank financial firms—leaving only the bad choice between disorderly bankruptcy and ad hoc bailout. You should:
- Establish precisely what legal tools you have (lender-of-last-resort, capital injection, guarantee, resolution/OLA-type authority). Insurers are often *exempt from ordinary bankruptcy* and regulated at a sub-national level, which complicates seizure of subsidiaries and cross-border operations.
- **Coordinate** the monetary authority (liquidity), the fiscal authority (capital/guarantees), and the insurance regulator(s). In AIG, fragmented supervision was a core weakness.
- Consider whether an orderly resolution framework applies; if a modern resolution regime (à la the FSB *Key Attributes* / BRRD-style "third option") is available, it may allow continuity of vital functions without an open-ended taxpayer bailout.

---

## Bottom line

I would **not** recommend simply granting the company's request for emergency liquidity on its own. Instead:

1. **Establish viability** and get inside the firm to size the true need.
2. **Push hard for a private solution** (funding or acquisition) first.
3. **Do nothing that risks a further downgrade**—avoid counterparty haircuts and punitive loan terms.
4. If public liquidity is warranted, provide it **freely, flexibly-collateralized, at a non-punitive rate, as an explicit bridge.**
5. **Pair or promptly follow it with structural measures**—capital injection and removal of the specific loss-driving exposures that are generating the collateral calls—to address the root cause.
6. **Protect taxpayers via collateral, seniority, an equity stake with upside, and governance rights**, keeping the structure legally robust; handle moral hazard through *ex-post* discipline, not punitive pricing.
7. **Confirm legal authority and coordinate** monetary, fiscal, and insurance-regulatory bodies throughout.

Handled this way—as the AIG precedent shows—a systemically important insurer can be stabilized, wound down in size, its obligations honored to avoid contagion, and public funds ultimately recouped.

*Caveat:* every crisis differs, decisions must be made fast and with imperfect information, and the specific mix of tools should be tailored to your jurisdiction's legal authorities and the firm's actual solvency. Document the options considered and the basis for decisions—you will face intense scrutiny after the fact.
