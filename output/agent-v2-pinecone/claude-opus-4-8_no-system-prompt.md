# MEMO: Options for an Emergency Broad-Based Lending Program

**To:** Supervisor
**Re:** Design options for an emergency liquidity program supplementing existing standing facilities
**Basis:** Yale Program on Financial Stability survey, *Broad-Based Emergency Liquidity Programs* (Wiggins, Fulmer, Feldberg & Metrick, 2022), synthesizing 33 historical programs across 10 jurisdictions, plus related YPFS case material.

---

## 1. Framing: what kind of program we are designing

What you've described — a broad-based program available to all banks, supplementing existing standing facilities, deployed in the acute phase of a run that is spreading — fits squarely within what the literature calls a **Broad-Based Emergency Liquidity (BBEL)** program. The defining features match our situation:

- **Broad-based**: available to a wide range of institutions, not a single firm (that would be "ad hoc emergency liquidity," a different tool — appropriate if we needed to rescue *only* the one G-SIB).
- **Emergency**: deployed during recognized systemwide stress, as a deliberate supplement to standing facilities (e.g., the discount window).

The survey's central reframing of Bagehot's dictum should anchor everything we do:

> *"In the acute (panic) phase of a crisis, a LOLR should lend freely and broadly against good collateral, at rates set by auction, taking care to avoid disclosing details about individual borrowers."*

Note three deliberate departures from the classic dictum that are directly relevant to your assignment: (1) explicit emphasis on lending **broadly**; (2) **auction-set rates** rather than a flat penalty rate; and (3) **minimal individual-borrower disclosure** during the acute phase. The authors also pointedly omit any "solvent institutions only" requirement — more on that below.

---

## 2. The master trade-off: moral hazard vs. stigma

Every design decision turns on one tension:

- **Moral hazard concerns** push toward penalty rates, restricted collateral, restricted counterparties, and caps.
- **Stigma concerns** push the other way: punitive, restrictive terms signal that any user must be in trouble, which suppresses participation — and an emergency program nobody uses fails at its only job (arresting the run).

**The historical record is emphatic that over-restriction is the more common failure mode in the acute phase.** Cautionary examples:

- The **UK Discount Window Facility** charged a high penalty rate and required *on-the-day* solvency/viability tests. It was **never drawn** — too stigmatizing to be useful.
- The Bank of England's autumn-2007 penalty-rate term repos had **no takers**; once reformatted as the **ELTR** (penalty removed), usage was robust.
- The Fed's **discount window** went underused in 2007 despite rate cuts, purely because of stigma — which is precisely why the Fed built the **Term Auction Facility (TAF)** as a destigmatized substitute.

The headline lesson: **early deployment of *credible* assistance can arrest a crisis before it becomes chronic.** Given you already have a spreading run, speed and uptake matter more than guarding against moral hazard, which can be addressed later (in the chronic phase, and via ex-post disclosure).

## 3. The key design decisions (your menu of options)

Below I organize the survey's 20 "key design decisions" into the choices you actually need to put in front of leadership.

### A. Legal authority — decide this first, it constrains speed
The survey groups programs into four authority types. In descending order of preference for *speed*:

1. **Modify/expand an existing standing facility under regular authority** (12 of 33 cases). Fastest path — lower the rate, lengthen maturities, broaden collateral, widen eligibility, increase frequency. The BoE ran *most* of its GFC response this way; the ECB's same-day FTOs are the extreme example of speed.
2. **Invoke a preexisting *emergency* authority** (7 cases) — e.g., a statutory power that activates on a finding of "severe/unusual stress" (Bank of Canada §18(g)), or a pre-positioned contingency facility (UK ECTR, designed in 2011, activated 2012). **Strong recommendation:** check whether we have such pre-positioned authority; if not, this is a lesson for peacetime.
3. **Seek new legislation** (6 cases) — slowest, and "lengthy, drawn-out debates" undermine market confidence. Avoid if possible in the acute phase.
4. **Opaque authority** (8 cases, mostly 19th-c. clearinghouses) — not an option for a modern central bank.

> **Action:** Start from existing discount-window / OMO infrastructure. It's faster, leverages prescreened counterparties and known collateral schedules, and aids our own risk management. Reserve new legislation for the chronic phase if needed.

### B. Rate setting — the single most consequential choice
Three structures:

- **Auction (recommended for the acute phase).** This was *"the most important innovation of the GFC-era programs."* An auction yields a market-determined (effectively penalty) rate *while minimizing stigma* — borrowing at auction looks like market borrowing, not like begging at a penalty window. It also gives us real-time pricing information on the severity of stress. The Fed TAF is the model: minimum bid below interbank rate but above OIS.
  - Sub-choice: **uniform vs. discriminatory pricing.** Uniform pricing (every winner pays the lowest accepted bid) discourages defensive underbidding and is simpler to communicate; the BoE moved to uniform pricing in the ILTR for exactly this reason. Discriminatory pricing extracts truer valuations and resists collusion. For a panic, I'd lean **uniform**.
- **Fixed rate.** Administratively simple but dangerous: overprice → stigma and no use (UK DWF); underprice → moral hazard and overuse. Useful mainly for on-demand bilateral facilities and as a natural exit mechanism.
- **Fixed-rate, full-allotment ("unlimited").** Reserve for the *most critical* moments. The ECB switched TROs to fixed-rate full-allotment after Lehman; the Fed effectively did the same with TAF. A senior ECB official called it *"the most significant non-standard measure"* the bank deployed. Given your run is already spreading, this should be **on the table as an escalation option.**

### C. Eligible participants & the solvency question
- **Default: banks (your existing supervised counterparties).** You retain supervisory knowledge and minimize information asymmetry. The consensus is banks should have generous access "so long as they are solvent and viable," while nonbanks should face "constructive ambiguity."
- **Foreign banks:** A real decision. The Greek ELA excluded non-Greek-owned banks, which pushed a distressed foreign branch elsewhere and contributed to a parent's liquidation. By contrast, the Fed TAF *did* admit US branches/subsidiaries of foreign banks — who became ~65% of borrowing. For a G-SIB-centered crisis with cross-border linkages, consider including locally-incorporated foreign-bank subsidiaries.
- **Solvency/viability tests — handle with great care.** The survey is direct: distinguishing illiquid from insolvent in the heat of a panic is *"difficult and even counterproductive,"* and "any strict solvency condition is doomed to failure" in the acute phase. On-the-day testing destroyed the UK DWF's usability. The restated dictum **deliberately omits** a solvency requirement. **Recommendation: do not impose hard solvency gates in the acute phase.** Solvency is an equity problem that lending cannot fix; address it later with fiscal tools (recapitalization, asset purchases) in the chronic phase.

### D. Collateral
- **Lend secured, with haircuts** (32 of 33 cases were secured). Bagehot's "good collateral" means collateral that was good *before the panic* — don't mark to fire-sale prices.
- **Borrow your existing standing-facility collateral schedule and haircuts** for speed and fairness, but **build in administrative flexibility to broaden** it. Eight cases expanded collateral mid-program. The RFC's overly narrow initial collateral *raised* borrower failure rates until loosened.
- **Protective tools:** currency add-on haircuts (ECB +8% non-euro; BoE +3% non-sterling), own-name surcharges, and **overcollateralization cushions** (TAF required up to 100%, later 33%, held on deposit).
- **Consider collateral-differentiated pools** (BoE ILTR's narrow/wide sets) — this lets pricing reveal *where* stress is concentrated and acts as an early-warning indicator.

### E. Maturity
Liquidity stress shortens maturities, so **offer term funding longer than your overnight standing facility** (the most common single move across all cases). Offer multiple tenors (e.g., 1 / 3 months, extendable). Longer terms raise credit risk — manage via overcollateralization (as TAF did when adding 84-day loans).

### F. Size & individual limits
- Central banks usually **do not pre-announce an aggregate cap**; instead they set per-auction amounts and **increase them in response to demand** — a "very powerful tool" in the acute phase. The BoE, ECB, and Fed all scaled up in 2008.
- **Per-borrower caps** (e.g., TAF's 10% of any auction) serve double duty: they limit moral hazard *and* protect anonymity (no one can infer who borrowed what), reducing stigma.

### G. Stigma strategy (treat as a first-order design goal, not an afterthought)
Proven techniques, all visible in TAF:
1. Many participants;
2. Auction pricing;
3. Per-borrower allotment limits;
4. Delayed settlement (TAF settled 3 days later — inconsistent with a failing firm's needs);
5. **Minimal individual disclosure during the acute phase.** Disclosure of borrower identities reliably triggers runs (NYCH 1890; the RFC's forced public reading of borrower names accelerated deposit flight at "revealed" banks). Report **aggregate** data now; provide borrower-level detail only after markets calm (which also serves taxpayer accountability and mitigates moral hazard).

### H. Communication & timing
- Announce clearly, consistently, and **only when the program is operationally ready** — failing to deliver promised liquidity is worse than not promising (cf. Overend-Gurney 1866). Inconsistent messaging actively worsened the 1965 Hong Kong run.
- A credible announcement can itself calm a panic with little drawdown ("announcement effect" — Y2K SFF: $481bn of options sold, *none* exercised; Canada TLF; BoE 1866).
- Pre-position language allowing later expansion (sizes, maturities) so you can adjust administratively without re-announcing.

### I. Exit strategy (design it in now)
Four mechanisms, often combined:
1. **Pricing** — a premium rate naturally depresses usage as markets recover (cleanest exit);
2. **Expiration date** (signals "temporary," extendable);
3. **Wind-down** by shrinking auction size/frequency (how the Fed exited TAF);
4. **Transition to a standing facility** — if the tool proves valuable, fold it permanently into the framework (UK kept ECTR and ILTR; Hong Kong kept two of five measures). If you do this, ensure peacetime pricing is unattractive vs. market funding.

## 4. Recommended package (for discussion)

Given an active, spreading run originating at a G-SIB, I'd put forward a **term auction facility built on existing infrastructure**, specifically:

1. **Authority:** modify the existing standing facility / use any pre-positioned emergency authority — avoid the legislative path now.
2. **Structure:** term, uniform-price auction; minimum bid below interbank rate, above the floor rate; multiple tenors (1 & 3 months), extendable.
3. **Eligibility:** all supervised banks; include locally-incorporated foreign-bank subsidiaries; **no hard solvency gate** in the acute phase.
4. **Collateral:** adopt the standing-facility schedule and haircuts, with administrative authority to broaden; apply overcollateralization and own-name/foreign-currency add-ons.
5. **Size:** per-auction amounts scalable on demand; **pre-authorize escalation to fixed-rate, full-allotment** if the run intensifies.
6. **Stigma controls:** per-borrower caps (~10%), delayed settlement, aggregate-only disclosure during the acute phase.
7. **Companion measures:** be ready, with the fiscal authority, to deploy **deposit/liability guarantees** in the acute phase and **recapitalization/asset purchases** in the chronic phase — liquidity alone cannot solve solvency. Multiple, layered BBEL programs (different maturities, collateral, participant classes) are common if the constraint persists (Canada ran three; UK four).
8. **Exit:** premium pricing + stated (extendable) end date + planned wind-down; decide later whether to make it a standing facility.
9. **Coordination:** if the G-SIB and run have cross-border dimensions, coordinate announcements with peer central banks and consider FX swap lines — the GFC's coordinated multi-central-bank actions were notably effective.
