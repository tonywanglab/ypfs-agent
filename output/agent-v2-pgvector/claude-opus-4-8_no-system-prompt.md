# MEMO: Options for an Emergency Lending Program to Supplement Standing Facilities

## Framing the problem

Your situation maps almost exactly onto what the literature calls a **Broad-Based Emergency Liquidity (BBEL) program** — defined as an intervention that is (a) *broad-based*, targeted to a wide range of institutions rather than one firm, and (b) *emergency*, introduced during systemwide stress as a supplement to or modification of standing facilities like the discount window (Wiggins et al., *Broad-Based Emergency Liquidity Programs*, YPFS Survey, 2022).

Two things from the corpus are worth flagging immediately for your supervisor:

1. **You are in the "acute phase."** A run on a globally significant bank plus signs of contagion is the textbook acute phase, where liquidity stress is obvious but solvency is hard to judge in the moment. The survey is explicit that in this phase, policy should be *fast*, should *emphasize protection of fragile liabilities*, and that "any strict solvency condition is doomed to failure — there is simply no time to make such judgments."

2. **Liquidity alone may not be enough.** The corpus repeatedly warns that LOLR tools "are not alchemy. Liquidity support alone is a poor substitute for capital" (Geithner, *Reflections on the Lender of Last Resort*). A BBEL program is the right *first* response, but you should advise your supervisor that guarantees and/or capital may be needed if the trouble proves to be about solvency, not just liquidity. More on this in the final section.

## The single most important strategic lesson: deploy early and credibly

The survey's first and most emphasized theme: **"Early deployment of credible BBEL assistance in the acute phase of a crisis can serve to arrest or moderate the crisis and stop it from evolving into an extended chronic phase."**

Geithner reinforces this: *"The typical crisis starts slowly but can accelerate dramatically. You need to try to be preemptive. If you establish the backstop after the run has too much momentum, then it will not be effective."*

**Recommendation:** Move now, while you still can shape expectations.

---

## The central design trade-off you must manage: moral hazard vs. stigma

Every design decision below is governed by one tension the survey identifies as "to some extent unavoidable":

- **Moral hazard concerns** push toward penalty rates, restricted collateral, and limits.
- **Stigma concerns** push the other way: punitive terms signal that any user "must be in some trouble," which depresses participation and renders the program ineffective at stopping a panic.

The survey's headline conclusion — a modern restatement of Bagehot's dictum — is your guiding principle:

> **"In the acute (panic) phase of a crisis, a LOLR should lend freely and broadly against good collateral, at rates set by auction, taking care to avoid disclosing details about individual borrowers."**

Note pointedly that the restated dictum says *nothing* about lending only to "solvent" institutions — the authors state "we did that on purpose."

## OPTION SET — concrete program designs

The survey shows that the fastest and most efficient programs **rely on existing authorities, programs, or administrative frameworks** (its second major theme). I have ordered the options from fastest/least novel to most novel.

### Option A — Enhance your existing standing facilities (fastest first move)
This is what most central banks did first. Using existing legal authority, you can:
- **Lower the borrowing rate**
- **Lengthen maturities** (the most common move — e.g., from overnight to 1–3 months)
- **Broaden eligible collateral**
- **Increase frequency and capacity** of operations

*Precedents:* The Hong Kong Monetary Authority did all of this in 2008. The Bank of England's Extended-Collateral Long-Term Repo was simply an adjustment to scheduled operations requiring no new legislation. **Caution flag from the corpus:** Simply expanding the discount window often fails because of **stigma** — the Fed lowered the primary credit rate in August 2007 and borrowing remained limited. This is precisely why a separate, auction-based facility (Option B) was created.

### Option B — A new term auction facility (the strongly favored model) ⭐
This is the design the survey highlights most favorably. The Fed's **Term Auction Facility (TAF)** was explicitly built to deliver discount-window-style funding *without* stigma. Its destigmatizing features are worth replicating:
- **Auction format** ("no institution was being forced to borrow"; rates appear market-determined, not penalty rates)
- **Many simultaneous participants**, so identities can't be inferred
- **Per-bidder caps** (no bidder could take more than 10% of a TAF auction) to guarantee broad participation and anonymity
- **Delayed settlement** (3 days), inconsistent with the needs of a failing institution
- **A minimum bid rate** set below the interbank rate but above the overnight indexed swap rate

The survey calls the auction mechanism "the most important innovation of the GFC-era programs." Evidence it worked: more than half of TAF participants paid a *premium* over the discount window rate just to avoid DW stigma.

### Option C — Multiple complementary facilities (if stress persists/broadens)
The survey's third theme: "If the liquidity constraint persists, we often see the use of multiple BBEL programs to provide wide access to a broad range of participants." The Bank of Canada ran three programs to fill gaps across (i) different maturities, (ii) different collateral classes, and (iii) different participant types. You can launch one broad program now and pre-position others.

## KEY DESIGN DECISIONS — recommendations for each parameter

**1. Legal authority.** Confirm whether you can act under existing/standing authority. The survey strongly advises this for speed. If new legislation is needed, note the warning: "time is of the essence. Lengthy, drawn-out debates are unlikely to provide a sense of confidence." Several jurisdictions (UK, Hong Kong) had *pre-positioned* contingency facilities that the governor could trigger — a model worth noting for the future.

**2. Eligible participants.** Since the program should be "broadly available to all banks," start with the full set of solvent banks. The consensus view: banks should have generous access; nonbank financial institutions should "face constructive ambiguity." If you want to reach NBFIs without taking on their unknown risk, consider the **Bank of Canada solution**: let them access the facility *indirectly through primary dealers* with whom you already have relationships.

**3. Solvency/viability tests.** **Avoid an on-the-day solvency test.** The cautionary case is the **UK Discount Window Facility**, whose on-the-day solvency-and-viability test (combined with a high penalty rate) contributed to its *never being drawn* throughout its existence. The acute phase leaves no time for such judgments.

**4. Rates.** Use an **auction** to set rates (uniform pricing reduces incentives to underbid relative to true need, as the BoE found with its ILTR). If you must use a fixed rate, beware: the survey documents programs priced so high they got "no takers" (the BoE's autumn 2007 term repos), and only saw usage once the penalty was removed. "A central bank can price itself out of the market."

**5. Program size.** You need not pre-announce an aggregate cap. Crucially, **retain flexibility to increase auction sizes in response to demand** — the survey calls this "a very powerful tool, especially in the acute phase." In the *most critical* situations (post-Lehman), both the ECB and the Fed moved to **fixed-rate, full-allotment** (unlimited) provision. The ECB's executive board termed this "the most significant non-standard measure" it implemented. Given you have a G-SIB run with spreading contagion, full-allotment is a live option — but the survey notes the trade-off: a very large/unlimited program has a strong announcement effect but risks signaling "the situation is worse than it is."

**6. Collateral.** Lend against good collateral, but define it broadly enough to be useful. **Caution:** the **Reconstruction Finance Corporation** initially accepted too-narrow collateral, which impeded usage and *raised borrower failure rates*; broadening it lowered failures. Build in administrative flexibility to widen collateral as conditions evolve. Apply established haircut schedules for speed and fairness.

**7. Maturity.** Offer longer maturities than your standing overnight facilities, since a hallmark of the crisis is that available maturities shorten.

**8. Disclosure / stigma.** During the acute phase, **disclose only aggregate data, not individual borrower identities.** The corpus contains a clean natural experiment: when RFC borrower names were read aloud, "revealed banks lost deposits faster and were more likely to be closed." Northern Rock's leaked discount-window borrowing triggered a depositor run. Provide individual-level transparency only *after* markets calm (this also serves taxpayers and addresses moral hazard later).

**9. Communication.** Be clear, consistent, and confident; a credible announcement can itself calm a run (the 1866 Bank of England note-issuance announcement calmed markets without the power ever being used). Conversely, inconsistent messaging (Hong Kong 1965) actively undermined the intervention. Complete operational setup *before* announcing.

**10. Exit strategy.** Build in a natural exit via **pricing** (premium rates become unattractive as markets normalize) and/or an expiration date, with the option to wind down by shrinking auction sizes. Consider whether a successful temporary facility should later become a *standing* facility (as the UK and Hong Kong did with several programs).

## Critical caveat to give your supervisor: liquidity may not be sufficient

Because the troubled institution is a *globally significant bank* and contagion is already visible, you should explicitly prepare your principals for the possibility that this is not purely a liquidity problem. The corpus is emphatic:

- "In a fragile financial system, where solvency is in question, the failure of even relatively modestly sized institutions can lead to runs on the relatively strong."
- The survey's fourth theme: BBEL programs are commonly deployed *alongside* **credit and account guarantees in the acute phase**, and **asset purchases, recapitalizations, and loan guarantees in the chronic phase.**
- Geithner's core lesson: "The mix of funding, guarantees, and capital… The absence of any one of these tools cannot practically be compensated for by the presence of the others."

**Recommendation:** Launch the BBEL program now as the first line of defense, but in parallel begin coordinating with the fiscal authority on **liability guarantees** and contingency planning for capital, since these lie outside most central banks' authority.

## Bottom-line recommendations

1. **Act preemptively and credibly now** — before the run gains momentum.
2. **Launch an auction-based term facility (Option B)** modeled on the TAF, using existing authority where possible, with anti-stigma features (auction pricing, many participants, per-bidder caps, delayed settlement, aggregate-only disclosure).
3. **Make it broadly available to all solvent banks; avoid an on-the-day solvency test;** consider indirect access for systemic NBFIs via dealers.
4. **Retain flexibility to scale up** auction sizes — and keep **fixed-rate full-allotment** in reserve for the most acute scenario.
5. **Coordinate in parallel with the fiscal authority** on guarantees/capital, recognizing liquidity alone may not stop a solvency-driven run.
6. **Consider international coordination** — the survey documents that the most effective GFC responses (e.g., the December 2007 seven-central-bank announcement) were coordinated, given your bank is globally significant.
