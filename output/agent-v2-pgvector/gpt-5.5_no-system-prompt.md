## Emergency broad-based bank liquidity program options

### Executive view

Given a run at a globally significant domestic bank and signs of contagion, the central bank should consider a **broad-based emergency liquidity facility for banks**, not a program framed around the troubled institution. The objective should be to reassure depositors, short-term creditors, and banks that **systemwide liquidity is available against sound collateral**, while minimizing stigma and avoiding the impression that any one bank is being rescued.

A practical recommendation would be:

1. **Immediately announce a broad-based term liquidity facility for all eligible banks.**
2. Use an **auction-based design** as the first option, to reduce stigma and reveal demand.
3. Prepare a **fixed-rate full-allotment backstop** if the run intensifies or auction capacity proves insufficient.
4. Broaden eligible collateral, with conservative haircuts, but avoid unsecured lending except with fiscal indemnity.
5. Disclose only aggregate usage during the acute phase; delay borrower-level disclosure until conditions normalize or as legally required.

# Option 1: Term Auction Facility for Banks — recommended first-line supplement

### Description

Create a temporary facility through which eligible banks can obtain term central bank funding through regular auctions. This would be similar in spirit to the Federal Reserve’s **Term Auction Facility** during the global financial crisis.

### Core design

| Feature | Suggested design |
|---|---|
| Eligible borrowers | All domestically supervised banks with access to standing facilities; consider including domestic branches/subsidiaries of foreign banks if legally and operationally feasible |
| Form | Collateralized loan or repo |
| Maturities | 28-day and 84-day terms initially; option to add 3- and 6-month terms |
| Frequency | Weekly auctions initially; increase frequency if funding stress rises |
| Pricing | Uniform-price auction with a minimum bid rate above policy rate but below stressed market funding rates |
| Collateral | Same as standing facility at launch, with authority to expand rapidly |
| Haircuts | Existing collateral schedule plus add-ons for lower-quality, illiquid, own-name, or foreign-currency collateral |
| Individual cap | Limit each borrower to a percentage of each auction, e.g., 10–20%, to avoid concentration and reduce stigma |
| Settlement | Not immediate — e.g., T+1 to T+3 — to signal that this is not only for failing institutions |
| Disclosure | Publish aggregate auction results, not borrower names during the acute phase |
| Exit | Reduce auction sizes and maturities as market conditions normalize |

### Advantages

- **Reduces stigma** compared with ordinary discount-window borrowing.
- Broad participation makes it harder to identify weak banks.
- Auction results reveal market demand for liquidity.
- The central bank controls the quantity supplied.
- Can be implemented using existing collateral and operational infrastructure.

### Disadvantages

- If panic is severe, fixed auction sizes may be seen as insufficient.
- Some banks may fail to obtain funds if auctions are oversubscribed.
- Requires credible communication that further auctions and larger sizes will be available if needed.

### Best use case

This is the best initial supplement if the central bank wants to provide broad liquidity while preserving some control over take-up and moral hazard.

# Option 2: Fixed-rate, full-allotment term refinancing facility — strongest anti-run option

### Description

Offer eligible banks as much term liquidity as they demand, against eligible collateral, at a fixed central bank rate. This resembles the European Central Bank’s fixed-rate full-allotment longer-term refinancing operations during the global financial crisis.

### Core design

| Feature | Suggested design |
|---|---|
| Eligible borrowers | Broad set of domestic banks |
| Form | Repo or collateralized loan |
| Maturities | Overnight, 1-month, 3-month, and 6-month; consider 12-month if wholesale rollover risk is severe |
| Pricing | Fixed rate: policy rate plus modest spread, or standing facility rate minus stigma premium |
| Quantity | Full allotment against eligible collateral |
| Collateral | Broad collateral set, including high-quality securities, covered bonds, government-guaranteed bank debt, ABS, performing loan pools, and possibly own-name securities with steep haircuts |
| Haircuts | Conservative, differentiated by asset class, maturity, liquidity, and currency |
| Disclosure | Aggregate only during acute phase |
| Exit | Raise spread gradually, shorten maturities, and reduce collateral breadth as conditions improve |

### Advantages

- Sends the clearest possible message: **liquidity will be available**.
- Can stop rollover fears and funding-market hoarding.
- Avoids failed or underfilled auctions.
- Useful when the objective is to arrest a broad run quickly.

### Disadvantages

- Greater balance-sheet expansion.
- Higher moral hazard risk.
- Harder to calibrate rate: too high creates stigma; too low encourages overuse.
- Requires strong collateral controls and, ideally, fiscal risk-sharing if collateral becomes risky.

### Best use case

Use if contagion is accelerating, interbank markets are freezing, or markets doubt the adequacy of limited auctions.

# Option 3: Extended-collateral term repo facility

### Description

Create a special repo or lending facility that accepts a wider set of collateral than ordinary standing facilities and provides term funding. This is useful if banks are liquid on a mark-to-maturity basis but are constrained because their usable collateral at existing facilities is limited.

### Core design

| Feature | Suggested design |
|---|---|
| Eligible borrowers | Banks already eligible for standing facilities |
| Maturities | 1-, 3-, and 6-month repos |
| Pricing | Auction-based or fixed spread over policy rate |
| Collateral | Broader than standing facility: high-quality securitizations, covered bonds, performing loan portfolios, investment-grade corporate credit, government-guaranteed bank debt |
| Haircuts | Conservative; additional haircut for less liquid, own-name, or foreign-currency collateral |
| Size | Either capped auctions or full allotment against eligible collateral |
| Exit | Narrow collateral eligibility as markets normalize |

### Advantages

- Directly addresses collateral bottlenecks.
- Can prevent forced asset sales.
- Makes existing standing-facility backstops more usable without permanently changing them.
- Can be framed as liquidity insurance rather than bank support.

### Disadvantages

- Valuation and risk management are harder.
- Expanding collateral too much can expose the central bank to credit losses.
- If too restrictive, usage may be low and credibility impaired.

### Best use case

Use when banks have assets but not enough collateral eligible at standing facilities, or when market haircuts have risen sharply.

# Option 4: Contingent term repo / market-wide stress facility

### Description

Activate or create a contingent facility that can be turned on during “actual or prospective market-wide stress.” This resembles the Bank of England’s contingent term repo framework.

### Core design

| Feature | Suggested design |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Governor/board finding of market-wide bank funding stress |
| Eligible borrowers | Banks and possibly other supervised deposit-taking institutions |
| Maturities | 1-, 3-, and 6-month terms |
| Pricing | Fixed rate or auction spread over policy rate |
| Collateral | Wide collateral set, possibly matching the widest standing collateral framework |
| Quantity | Initially capped; authority to move to unlimited full allotment |
| Communication | Emphasize systemwide liquidity insurance, not support for a particular bank |

### Advantages

- Clear legal and governance trigger.
- Strong signaling effect.
- Can be retained as a permanent contingency tool after the crisis.
- Useful if the central bank wants a facility separate from ordinary monetary policy operations.

### Disadvantages

- If introduced only after a crisis begins, markets may see it as reactive.
- May still carry stigma if participation is viewed as exceptional.
- Less effective than full allotment if markets are already in panic.

### Best use case

Good as a standing or semi-standing crisis tool, but in the present situation it should be paired with a concrete operational facility immediately.

# Option 5: Liquidity insurance options program — not ideal for the acute phase, but useful as add-on

### Description

Auction options that give banks the right to draw central bank liquidity at specified dates or under specified stress conditions. This can have a strong announcement effect.

### Advantages

- Encourages ex ante liquidity planning.
- Can calm markets even if not drawn.
- May reduce future stigma by making access pre-arranged.

### Disadvantages

- Too slow and indirect for an active bank run.
- More useful for foreseeable liquidity events than sudden panics.
- Requires careful pricing and operational setup.

### Best use case

Not the main response now, but worth considering later as part of the permanent liquidity insurance framework.

# Recommended package for the current scenario

## 1. Announce a broad-based term auction facility immediately

The central bank should state that it will conduct regular term funding auctions for all eligible banks, against eligible collateral, to support the functioning of bank funding markets and the flow of credit.

Suggested initial parameters:

- **Borrowers:** all solvent, supervised domestic banks eligible for standing facilities.
- **Maturities:** 28 days and 84 days.
- **Auction size:** large initial size; commit to increase if oversubscribed.
- **Pricing:** uniform-price auction with minimum bid rate modestly above the policy rate.
- **Collateral:** standing-facility collateral initially, with immediate expansion authority.
- **Disclosure:** publish aggregate auction results only.

## 2. Prepare escalation to fixed-rate full allotment

If the run spreads or auctions are materially oversubscribed, announce that future operations will be conducted at fixed rate with full allotment against eligible collateral.

This is the strongest way to eliminate rollover fears.

## 3. Expand collateral eligibility

The facility should accept a broader collateral pool than the standing facility, subject to conservative haircuts. Possible additions:

- government and agency securities;
- covered bonds;
- high-quality securitizations;
- investment-grade corporate bonds;
- performing residential mortgage loans;
- performing commercial and consumer loan pools;
- government-guaranteed bank debt.

Avoid unsecured lending unless backed by a fiscal indemnity.

## 4. Maintain confidentiality during the acute phase

To reduce stigma:

- disclose aggregate usage only;
- publish auction statistics but not borrower identities;
- delay institution-level disclosure until after market conditions stabilize, subject to legal requirements.

## 5. Separate liquidity support from solvency intervention

The broad-based program should not be the only policy tool if the globally significant bank is insolvent or near-insolvent. Emergency liquidity can address runs and fire sales, but solvency problems require other tools: resolution, capital injection, asset guarantees, merger support, or fiscal backstop.

# Key design choices for decision-makers

## Eligibility

Recommendation: include all supervised banks with access to existing facilities. Consider foreign-bank branches only if their inclusion is needed to stabilize domestic funding markets and legal risks are manageable.

Avoid designing the program around the troubled global bank. The facility should be demonstrably broad-based.

## Rate

Use a rate that is not punitive. A severe penalty rate can worsen stigma and suppress usage. A modest premium over the policy rate or an auction-determined rate with a floor is preferable.

## Collateral

“Good collateral” should mean assets that were sound before the panic, not necessarily assets currently trading at distressed fire-sale prices. Use conservative haircuts rather than excluding too much collateral.

## Size

Initial auction sizes should be large enough to be credible. If demand exceeds supply, increase auction size promptly. In a severe panic, move to full allotment.

## Communication

The announcement should be clear, confident, and operationally specific:

- why the facility exists;
- who can use it;
- what maturities are offered;
- what collateral is eligible;
- when the first operation occurs;
- that the central bank is prepared to adjust size, frequency, maturity, and collateral as needed.

Avoid ambiguous or inconsistent statements about whether support is limited.

## Exit

Build in an exit strategy:

- temporary authorization;
- periodic review;
- pricing that becomes unattractive once markets normalize;
- gradual reduction in auction frequency and size;
- narrowing of eligible collateral;
- option to incorporate useful features into the permanent liquidity framework.

# Bottom-line recommendation

For the supervisor, I would present **Option 1 — a broad-based term auction facility — as the preferred immediate program**, with **Option 2 — fixed-rate full allotment — as the escalation tool** if contagion worsens.

A concise recommended formulation:

> “The central bank should establish a temporary, broad-based term liquidity facility for all eligible banks, offering 28- and 84-day collateralized funding through uniform-price auctions, with the ability to increase auction size, extend maturities, broaden collateral, and move to fixed-rate full allotment if market-wide stress intensifies. Borrower-level information should not be disclosed during the acute phase. The facility should be framed as systemwide liquidity insurance, not support for any individual institution.”
